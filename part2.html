Why can't more data fix it?

Hyongdoo Tom Jang, PhD · October 2026

Part 1 ended with the rows stopping near ten thousand. The obvious reply is to wait for more. This part is about why that reply often misreads the problem. What limits a decision-level model is usually not the count of rows but their coverage: which cells of the decision space they land in. Rows added by the same operating policy land in the same cells.

It was never the count

Take the grid from Part 1 and keep adding rows the way a site actually adds them, each cause at its usual level most of the time. Under that illustrative comfort-zone distribution, ten times the rows you can get buys a quarter of the space, and a hundred times buys half. The far corners, where every cause is at an extreme at once, need about 170 million stopes to be seen once. No mine will record them, and those corners are where the costly surprises live.

Exhibit 1ANALYTICAL RESULT, ILLUSTRATIVE DISTRIBUTION
Ten times the rows buys a quarter of the space. The corners are never reached
Expected share of 59,049 cells reached at least once, against the number of rows, for rows drawn from the comfort zone and for rows spread evenly
Illustrative comfort-zone distribution: each of ten causes at its mid level with probability 0.7 and at either extreme with probability 0.15. The dashed line is the same count spread evenly, which no operation produces. The 170 million figure holds under this distribution, not in general.
coverage(n) = 1 − 1|C| Σc (1 − pc)n

A cell with probability pc needs about 1/pc rows to be seen once. For a cell where all ten causes sit at an extreme, pc = 0.15¹⁰, and 1/pc is 1.7 × 10⁸. The count is not the lever. Where the rows fall is.

The questions are outside the supported region

Coverage has a shape, and the shape is the figure at the top of this page. Operational data is sampled from what the site has been comfortable doing, not from the space of possible decisions. Plot a thousand past stopes on any two axes and they form a tilted cloud: aggressive designs where the ground was good, conservative ones where it was poor. The questions that need a model are often outside that cloud: pushing a design further, opening a new ground class, the first stope at depth.

Call the cloud the supported region: where the rows provide evidence. Inside it a model interpolates and the usual safeguards work. Outside it, a conventional supervised model can remain confidently wrong, because nothing in the training rows told it which way the world bends beyond them. Models built to carry uncertainty, a Gaussian process or a well-calibrated ensemble, widen their bands out there; most models in production do not.

The dashed outline in the figure is a convex hull, the simplest geometric picture of empirical support. In real problems with ten or more dimensions it is too strict: ordinary new rows fall outside it. Distance, density or domain-specific measures of support are usually more useful, and the point survives the change of measure.

x* ∉ conv{x1, …, xn}
dM(x*) = √((x* − μ)ᵀ Σ−1 (x* − μ))

A question outside the supported region is an extrapolation whatever the model. The Mahalanobis distance from the data cloud is one of the cheapest first screens, and the same statistic used to find outliers; here it finds the questions. It works best when the cloud is roughly elliptical; multimodal or strongly nonlinear data need density- or manifold-based measures instead.

The data is confounded by the engineer

The tilt of the cloud is the second problem. Engineers adapt the design to the ground: more support where it is poor, less where it is good. The data then shows more support alongside more dilution, and a model that was never told why the support was added can learn a spurious association in which more support predicts more dilution. Within any one ground class the effect runs the other way.

Prediction is not causation, and operational policy is part of the data-generating process. This is not a modelling error that more rows repair: every new row is generated by the same adaptive practice, so every new row carries the same association.

Omitted-variable bias. The second term does not shrink with n.

The data is missing dimensions the experts have

The third problem is the quietest. Anything worth predicting has something like a hundred causes. Ten of them are measured and become columns; most of the rest never enter the table and show up as noise. They are not noise to the engineer who has walked the drive. Expert knowledge and the regression are therefore not two versions of the same information. They are different information, and the part the expert holds is often the part that decides what happens outside the supported region.

Where the information a prediction needs actually lives
y = f(x1 … x10, z1 … z90) + ε

The z are unmeasured. To the model they are part of ε. To the expert they are the explanation.

Where machine learning does belong

None of this is an argument against machine learning. It is an argument for pointing it at the right layer. Most failed projects are misdiagnosed: a decision-level problem treated as a perception problem because the raw data was big. Thirty seconds of classification before any modelling saves most of them.

Nor is the decision layer closed to deep learning. Physics-informed networks, neural operators and digital twins learn the equations and the rows together; they are grey-box models with a neural residual, and they belong between the points as much as gradient boosting does. What they share with every method that works here is that the physics enters the fit. That is Part 3.

Most problems on a site are rows one to four and have good tools. The value and the risk sit in the last row, and so do most of the failures.

The next thousand rows land where the first thousand did. The missing information is in the people who made them.

If the rows cannot cover the question, the prediction must draw on information that is not contained in the rows. Part 3 asks what that information is, and how to put it inside the model.

Why does every engineering prediction run out of data?

Hyongdoo Tom Jang, PhD · October 2026

A surgeon, a farmer, a venture investor and a mining engineer will never sit at the same table. But stack up every outcome each has seen of one kind, and the stacks come out the same height: a few hundred to a few thousand. Not the same data. The same size.

Outcomes are counted per decision

An outcome arrives once per decision: one per stope, one per operation, one per harvest, one per launch. So the decision-level rows you will ever hold are bounded by three things: how often you decide, how long the process stays the same, and how many genuinely comparable units you can pool. In many high-value decision problems the usable count is measured in hundreds or thousands, not millions, and more sensors do not change it.

n ≈ r · T · U
rdecisions per year, bounded by throughput Tyears before the process changes, bounded by drift Ucomparable units you can pool, bounded by heterogeneity

Decision-level data is bounded by throughput, process drift and the number of genuinely comparable operating units. None of the three grows with the number of sensors.

What about the majors, and the fleets?

A large miner has fired hundreds of thousands of blasts. An autonomous haulage fleet logs a hundred variables a second for years. Neither contradicts the count, because the count is about rows of one kind: an outcome observed once per decision, with the same definition, from the same process. Pool thirty sites and twenty years and the rows go up, but so does heterogeneity, which is the U term: you now have thirty populations, and reconciling them is a knowledge problem of its own. Haul cycles, truck telemetry and shift logs are large because their outcome is recorded automatically; they are a different kind of problem with different tools (Part 2 has a thirty-second test). The claim is not that mines lack data. It is that the rows which answer a design question are few, in most operations.

A million samples, one row

Picture the table a decision-level model learns from. Each row is a stope, each column a property of it, the last column what happened. Every sensor on site writes into that table. None of them writes a row. The scan becomes one number, the drill log one number, the waveforms one number. A row is created by one event only: a decision made and its result observed.

The scope matters. For a time-series or condition-monitoring model, a window of readings can itself be a sample, and such models are rightly trained on millions. For a decision-level outcome model, a million readings still support one row.

The precision of a model that relates an outcome to its features scales with n, the number of rows. The number of readings behind each cell does not appear. Ten years of sensor data from a thousand stopes is a decision-level model of a thousand rows.

Ten thousand cannot fill ten dimensions

Ten thousand sounds like plenty, until you count the causes. Ten is modest for anything worth predicting, and three levels each is the coarsest grid there is: 3¹⁰ = 59,049 cells. Spread evenly, ten thousand rows would leave 84% of them empty. Ten thousand rows cannot uniformly cover a ten-dimensional decision space.

Operations do not spread evenly. They live in a comfort zone, each cause at its usual level most of the time. That is the figure at the top of this page: 4,028 cells reached, 93% of the space never observed, and the next decision often a question about the dark part. This is a coverage argument, not a claim that a model needs one row per cell; smoothness, sparsity and low-dimensional structure let models do far better than the grid suggests, inside the region the rows support.

This is not a property of mining or medicine. It is a property of the layer where decisions are made.

If the problem were only the number of rows, the obvious answer would be to collect more. Part 2 asks why that often fails.

How do you predict where the data isn't?

Hyongdoo Tom Jang, PhD · October 2026

Parts 1 and 2 left one route open. Outside the rows, a prediction has to come from somewhere else, and there are only two other places it can come from: the physics of the process, and the people who run it. Each of the three knows something the other two do not, and each fails in its own way. The job is not to pick one. It is to get all three into one model, in three steps.

Three sources of information, three failure modes, one model
Rows fail outside the supported region, physics fails where the equation is incomplete, experts fail by bias. Each covers the others

Three steps

Get the knowledge out of the experts, write it in a form a model can obey, and fit with it inside. Most projects do the first step once, in a workshop, and skip the other two. The value is in the third; the second is what makes the third possible.

What each step produces, and why the loop runs back to the start

1. Elicitation: get it out, and check it

A workshop produces opinions. Three checks turn them into knowledge: rounds, so the group converges and the questions that will not converge are flagged as real uncertainty; pairwise comparisons with a consistency check, so a self-contradicting set of answers is flagged for review or re-elicitation; and scoring on questions with known answers, so experts are weighted by how well they actually know, not by seniority.

Exhibit 3ILLUSTRATIVE MODEL
Three checks that turn opinions into knowledge
Delphi rounds, a consistency ratio on pairwise comparisons, and calibration on seed questions
Illustrative values. The consistency ratio compares a respondent's pairwise matrix to a random one; 0.1 is Saaty's heuristic threshold, a flag rather than a verdict. Calibration scores come from questions the site already has outcomes for.
CR = λmax − n(n − 1) · RI < 0.1
wi ∝ calibrationi × informationi

The first is Saaty's consistency test for a pairwise matrix. The second is a schematic of performance weighting in Cooke's classical model: experts who were right on the seed questions, and precise about it, carry more of the prior. The real method scores calibration with a likelihood-ratio statistic and information with an entropy measure; the idea is the same.

Experts are biased in documented ways. They anchor on the first number said aloud, remember the last incident more than the base rate, and say what the organisation wants to hear. The checks above are the first defence, weighting by calibration the second, and the loop at the end of this page the third. Expert judgement unchecked is not knowledge; it is another noisy sensor.

Each item on the list has a mathematical form. The form is the whole point: a sentence in a report changes nothing, and a constraint in the fit changes the prediction exactly where there are no rows.

3. Integration: fit with the knowledge inside

Where the knowledge enters decides whether it does anything. Applied to the model's output, as a rule system after the fact, it inherits the errors the model made outside the rows. Inside the fit, it decides what the model does there. The figure at the top of this page is a synthetic demonstration of the difference: the same seventy rows, three pieces of knowledge, and the error outside the rows falls from 70.6 to 1.2. The numbers belong to that example; the mechanism is general.

One line, and it is the whole of integration. Everything in the table above is a way of writing p(θ) that a particular model family can use.

Do not fix the weight

The common failure is a fixed blend: 60% model, 40% expert. It gives the experts too little say where the rows are absent and too much where they are dense. The weight has to be a function of where the question sits. A suitably structured Bayesian model does this naturally, through the prior; a weighted average of two finished predictions cannot.

Exhibit 7ANALYTICAL RESULT

With many rows nearby the data wins; with none, the prior carries the answer; τ is how firmly the experts hold it. Then close the loop: show the experts what the model learned and let them correct the prior. That is how a biased expert and a confounded dataset end up correcting each other instead of agreeing with each other.

How do you know the hybrid model is actually better?

Parts 1 and 2 said the failures happen in new regimes. A random train-test split never visits one, so it cannot see them. If the problem is extrapolation, the validation must contain extrapolation: hold out a whole ground class, a whole design regime or a whole mine, and ask whether the model held up where it had no rows, said so where it could not, and never bent against the physics.

If the problem is extrapolation, validation must also contain extrapolation.

Where hybrid models fail

Hybrid models do not remove uncertainty. Bad physics extrapolates confidently. Experts can share the same bias, and a Delphi round then converges on it. A constraint can be right in direction and wrong in magnitude. The purpose of integration is not to make extrapolation safe. It is to make the assumptions explicit, testable, and easier to refuse when the question leaves the domain. The goal is not always to predict; it is to know when not to.

The playbook

Five lines, for any outcome you need to predict

  1. List every cause you can think of, measured or not.
  2. Split them: measured become columns, the rest become the experts' list.
  3. Mark each one with its direction, and whether you control it.
  4. Fit a model that has to obey those directions, on the columns you have, over a physical base if one exists.
  5. Mark where the model is unsure, and let a person decide there.

Three ways out of the regime, worth checking first

  1. A simulator that can label the empty cells: pretrain on it, fine-tune on the rows.
  2. An experiment you are allowed to design: a few chosen trials cover more than a thousand records.
  3. A smaller question that stays inside the hull, answered by the rows alone.

What this series was about

Scarcity. The useful data are decision-level outcomes, not raw readings. Coverage. More rows from the same operating policy do not necessarily answer new decisions. Integration. When empirical support runs out, prediction needs explicit assumptions from physics and calibrated human knowledge, and a mechanism to refuse the question.

The goal is not to eliminate extrapolation. It is to know what supports it, what constrains it, and when to refuse it. Engineering AI fails not only when it has too little data, but when it is asked a question the data were never generated to answer.

The hardest prediction problems are not data problems. They are knowledge problems.
